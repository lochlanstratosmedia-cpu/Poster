# The Call Drill playbook

What the scoring and the training notes judge a call against. It combines
what leading real estate call trainers in Australia and the US teach with
published research on sales calls. Every technique names where it came from.
Numbers from Gong and Chorus are those vendors' analyses of their own
customers' call recordings, not peer-reviewed studies, so treat them as
strong hints rather than laws. Full notes and links are in
`docs/research/`.

## The ten rules

1. Have a reason for the call and say it early. A nearby sale, a new listing, their enquiry, their visit to the open. (Tom Panos, Ray White, Jeb Blount, Gong)
2. Talk less than half the time. Top performers in Gong's data sit near a 43:57 talk-to-listen ratio; average reps talk 65 to 70 percent. (Gong, Chorus)
3. Ask about why and where before when and how much. Motivation, destination, and timeline first. (Josh Phegan, Tom Ferry, Mike Ferry, Kevin Ward)
4. Spread real questions through the call. Gong's discovery data peaks at 11 to 14 questions per call, about problems and goals, not fired as a checklist. Chorus found more than two questions a minute hurts engagement. (Gong, Chorus)
5. Pause after an objection, then answer with a question. Gong's top reps pause about five times longer than average and reply with a question over half the time. (Gong, Tom Ferry, LabCoat Agents)
6. Acknowledge before you answer, and never make the prospect wrong. (Tom Ferry, Kevin Ward, Mike Ferry, Ninja Selling)
7. Sort a real objection from a delay tactic before you handle it. (Josh Phegan)
8. Prospect for a next step and a relationship, not the listing. Trust is built by frequency. (Tom Panos, Josh Phegan, Ricky Carruth)
9. Ask for a specific, dated next step and then stop talking. Gong found close rates fall sharply when no next step is agreed on the first call, and a specific time-and-date ask books more meetings than an open one. (Gong, Jeb Blount, Mike Ferry)
10. Do not sweat filler words. Gong found no link between "um" counts and win rates. Pace, calm, and interactivity matter more. (Gong, Chris Voss)

## Opening

- Five parts, in order: your name, your agency, the reason for the call, a "because" tied to their situation, then your question. Then stop talking. (Jeb Blount)
- "The reason for my call is..." is one of the strongest openers in Gong's cold-call data. A friendly "how have you been?" also outperformed. (Gong)
- Make the prospect comfortable in the first few seconds. Aim the call at what you can do for them, not what they can do for you. (Ricky Carruth)
- A warm call to someone who made contact (an enquiry, an open home visit) should name that contact in the first sentence: "You came through 12 Smith Street on Saturday." (Tom Ferry, Josh Phegan)
- Check it is a good time, or offer a fixed short slot: "Have you got two minutes, or should I call back at six?" (Brian Buffini, John McGrath)
- A cold call should aim for an opt-in, not a listing. Offer something small (the brochure, an invitation to the open, permission to tell them what it sells for) and ask for that. (Josh Phegan, Tom Panos)
- Do not read. Know the points and say them like a person. Tone and pace matter more than the exact words. (Brian Buffini, Ricky Carruth, Tom Ferry)

## Discovery

- Ask why they are moving before you ask when or how much. Motivation drives everything else. (Josh Phegan, Tom Ferry)
- The core buyer questions: where are they living now, do they own it, what is driving the move, what is the timing, who else is involved in the decision, and what has their lender told them not to go above. Ask them in order, do not skip. (Ninja Selling, Aaron Shiner, Tim and Julie Harris, Mike Ferry)
- The core vendor questions: where would they go, when, why now, what would stop them, have they spoken to other agents, and how they chose the last one. (Mike Ferry, Kevin Ward, Tom Ferry)
- Use question types on purpose. Situation questions establish facts. Problem questions surface what is not working. Implication questions get them to say what the problem costs. Need-payoff questions get them to describe the fix in their own words. Skilled sellers build the case before they ask for anything. (SPIN Selling, Neil Rackham)
- Go one layer deeper than the first answer: "Tell me more about that." "What have you tried?" "How long has that been the case?" "How do you feel about that?" (Sandler pain funnel)
- Find the gap between where they are and where they want to be. The bigger the gap they describe, the more motivated they are. (Gap Selling, Keenan)
- Calibrated questions that start with "how" or "what" give the prospect a sense of control and keep them talking. "What would need to be true for you to look at this again?" (Chris Voss)
- Ask about family, work, what they do for fun, and what they are hoping for. Listen for change, because change is what makes people move. (Ninja Selling)
- Let the prospect ask questions too. Chorus found the cold calls that convert have the prospect asking two or three questions of their own. (Chorus)

## Listening

- Reflect back what they said before you move on. Mirror the last few words ("...frustrated with the last campaign?") and label what you hear ("It sounds like you felt let down."). Aim for them to say "that's right." (Chris Voss)
- Follow the thread the prospect opened rather than the next question on your list. Questions spread across the call outperform a front-loaded checklist. (Gong)
- Keep any single stretch of your own talking short. Gong's guidance is under two and a half minutes; on a five minute call that means never more than a few sentences at once. (Gong)
- Do not interrupt. Unsuccessful reps in Gong's objection data interrupt and speed up. (Gong)
- Listen for motivation, timeline, frustration, and objections before you offer an observation. (Tom Ferry)
- Use "and" to move from their point to yours, not "but," so the concern stays validated. (Mike Ferry)

## Objections

The general method, in order:

1. Pause. Let it land. (Gong)
2. Acknowledge it without arguing: "Fair enough." "That makes sense." "I'd feel the same." (Tom Ferry, Kevin Ward, feel-felt-found)
3. Sort a real objection from a delay tactic, and isolate it: "Is that the only thing holding you back, or is there something else?" (Josh Phegan, LabCoat Agents)
4. Ask a clarifying question before you respond. (Tom Ferry, Gong)
5. Respond with something specific and small, then check it landed: "Does that sit better?" (Tom Ferry, LabCoat Agents)
6. Loop back to a next step. (LabCoat Agents)

Name their likely objection before they raise it when you can see it coming: "You're probably sick of agents ringing this week." That is an accusation audit, and it takes the sting out. (Chris Voss)

A memorised first line for the common ones buys your brain a second. Have one ready for each. (Jeb Blount, "the ledge")

Specific objections:

- "Just looking" or "still thinking". Agree, then ask what they thought of the home and whether the price felt right for the area. Then find out if they are watching values or considering a move in the next 6 to 12 months. (Tom Ferry)
- "The price seems high". Ask what they are comparing it to and what they would put it at. Price is a conversation, not a defence. Gong found calls that win discuss price early and more than once, after value is on the table. (Gong, Tom Panos)
- "Don't call me" or "I don't want to be hassled". Offer one specific, low-effort thing instead of a relationship: send the comparable sales, a text when a similar home lists, one call in a month. Then ask which they would prefer. (Tom Panos, MRI Software)
- "We'll wait until next year". Agree it makes sense, then offer quiet pre-marketing now so there is nothing to lose: photos, copy, and a few active buyers introduced privately before anything goes public. (Tom Ferry, Aaron Shiner)
- "You agents are all the same". Do not argue and do not badmouth anyone. Acknowledge it, then ask what happened with the last campaign and how they felt about it. Then ask what they would need to see to feel differently. (Kevin Ward, Keller Williams, Chris Voss)
- "The last agent kept telling us to drop the price". Ask what the enquiry, inspection numbers, and buyer feedback looked like week by week. A price conversation only works when it rests on that reporting, and a vendor who never got the reporting has a fair grievance. (Josh Phegan, Aaron Shiner, John McGrath)
- "What would you do differently". Answer with process, not a price promise. Bring evidence: what competing homes are doing, what the buyer feedback said, what the reporting will look like every week. (Tom Panos)
- "The other agent sold more" or "the other agent is cheaper". Compete on value, not fee. There is only ever one cheapest agent. (Josh Phegan, Tom Panos, Claudio Encina)
- "I'm not selling". Agree, and say this is not about selling. Offer information about the area instead. (Mat Steinwede, Aaron Shiner)
- "I already have an agent". Offer a market update as a second opinion or benchmark. (MRI Software, Mike Ferry)
- "Bring me a buyer" from a private seller. Agree that finding buyers is your job, confirm they would pay an agent who brings one, then ask how long before they might want a strong agent on it. (Kevin Ward)

## Closing for the next step

- Always leave with a specific, dated next step. Gong found reps skip next steps in about a quarter of first meetings and close rates fall sharply when they do. (Gong)
- Ask with a choice of two: "Saturday at two or Sunday at eleven?" (Mike Ferry)
- After you ask, stop talking. (Jeb Blount, Tom Ferry)
- For a buyer after an open: call the next day and offer a second look, then a private appointment tomorrow rather than the next open. (Josh Phegan, Tom Ferry)
- For a vendor: frame the appraisal as a small free service so they can make better decisions, and end with "would that be ok?" (Josh Phegan, Client Collective)
- If they will not commit to the appointment, take a smaller yes: permission to send comparable sales, a callback day, an email address. A call that gets an email address is a success. (Tom Panos, Ricky Carruth, Josh Phegan)
- Pre-qualify before the appointment: who else needs to be there, how long have they owned it, what improvements, what are they hoping for. (Mike Ferry, John McGrath)
- Confirm the details back and say what happens next. A go-forward plan with named steps is the close. (Lee Woodward)
- Hard closing techniques backfire on bigger decisions. Rackham's research found traditional closes negatively correlated with success in larger sales. Build the case, then ask plainly. (SPIN Selling)

## Price and the difficult vendor

- A price conversation rests on reporting: enquiry, inspections, offers, buyer feedback, days on market, comparable sales, and a clear recommendation. If the reporting was done, the conversation is simple. (Josh Phegan, Aaron Shiner, John McGrath)
- Lead with data and showing counts, not opinion. Phone first, written follow-up after. (Real Geeks, Mike Ferry)
- "Meet the market" is context, not spin. Explain the changeover figure versus the sale figure. (Tom Panos)
- State the facts plainly, then be quiet until the vendor speaks. (Tom Ferry)
- "It's the process, not the promise of a price, that gets top dollar." (Tom Panos)

## Delivery

- Lower, slower, calm. (Chris Voss)
- Keep turns short and let the prospect switch in often. Interactive calls win. (Gong)
- Matching the prospect's pace and wording builds rapport. (Chris Voss, Jacob and colleagues 2011)
- Filler words do not predict outcomes. Do not fix "um" at the expense of sounding like a person. (Gong)
- Never sound like you are reading. Internalise the points and practise out loud, then role play regularly. Role play has a large measured effect on skill learning. (Tom Ferry, Kevin Ward, Fu and Li 2025)

## What each scenario in this app is testing

- Warm lead follow-up (level 1): name their enquiry in the first line, ask why this home and what is driving the move, uncover who else is involved and their timing, then book a specific inspection with a choice of two times and confirm how you will send the details. A strong call asks at least four open questions before proposing a time.
- Open home follow-up (level 2): call the next day, recall the visit, ask what they thought and how the price felt for the area, find out whether they are watching or moving in 6 to 12 months, acknowledge "just looking" instead of pushing, and close for a second look with a partner or a specific callback day or permission to send comparable sales.
- Expired listing (level 3): expect hostility and name it before they do, never criticise the last agent, ask what happened week by week and how they felt about it, ask where they were going and whether that still stands, answer "what would you do differently" with process and evidence, and close for a face to face appraisal at a specific day and time.
